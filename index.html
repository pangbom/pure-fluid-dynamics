Lift curve

Sweeps the angle of attack, lets the flow settle at each step, and averages lift (Cl) and drag (Cd). Look for where Cl stops climbing in a straight line while Cd shoots up: that is the stall. Higher quality and lower viscosity give a sharper stall.

This is a 2D simulation at low Reynolds number on a coarse grid. Expect the right shape (linear rise, stall, rising drag) rather than wind-tunnel-accurate numbers.

Learn

What you are looking at

A 2D slice of air (or any incompressible fluid) solved with the Navier–Stokes equations on your graphics card, many times per second. Each grid cell tracks velocity and pressure. Air cannot pile up, so wherever it is squeezed it must speed up, and the pressure field is what pushes it around obstacles.

Lift

A wing turns the oncoming air downwards. Air over the top speeds up and its pressure drops (blue in the Pressure view); air underneath slows and pressure rises (orange). The pressure difference pushes the wing up. Cl is lift divided by dynamic pressure and chord, so it does not depend on wind speed.

Stall

Raise the angle of attack and lift grows almost linearly, until the air can no longer follow the curved upper surface. It separates, a large swirling wake forms and drag climbs steeply. Real wings at high Reynolds numbers lose lift abruptly at around 15°. At the low Reynolds numbers of this simulation, and of insects and small drones, stall is gentler: lift flattens and starts to wobble as vortices shed, while drag keeps rising. Watch the smoke peel off the top of the wing, or run the lift curve.

Vortex shedding

Behind a blunt body the wake becomes unstable and sheds swirls alternately from top and bottom: a Kármán vortex street. It is why wires sing in the wind and why flags flap. Look at the Vorticity view: red spins one way, teal the other.

Rotor behind a ridge

Wind crossing a sharp ridge separates on the lee side and rolls into a rotor: a region of reversed, turbulent flow near the ground. The Ridge scene shows why landing in the lee of a mountain on a windy day is a bad idea for pilots and skydivers.

Reynolds number

Re compares inertia to viscosity: speed times size divided by viscosity. Low Re flow is smooth and syrupy; high Re flow is turbulent. Real wings fly at Re in the millions. This simulation runs in the thousands, and numerical smoothing lowers it further, so treat the numbers as qualitative.

Views

Smoke shows where air from the inlet travels. Particles show individual parcels of air. Speed colours the flow from still (black) through blue and red to yellow. Pressure shows Cp: suction in blue, stagnation in orange, with isobars. Vorticity shows spin.
